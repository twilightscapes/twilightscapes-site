The sky rotates — fifteen degrees every hour. Your shutter decides whether that rotation reads as **pinpoint stars** or **trails of light**, and both are gorgeous, but you must choose.

**For pinpoint stars** (the Milky Way as your eye imagines it): keep the shutter short enough that the rotation doesn't smear. The classic guide is the **500 rule** — divide 500 by your lens's focal length. A 24mm lens: 500 ÷ 24 ≈ 20 seconds. A 14mm: about 35 seconds. Go past that and stars stretch into tiny dashes. Open the aperture wide, raise ISO to compensate, and focus at infinity — on a star, magnified, not by trusting the lens marking.

**For star trails** (circles sweeping around the pole star): you want the opposite — as much rotation as possible. The old way was a single exposure of thirty minutes or more, which cooks the sensor with noise. The modern way is **stacking**: dozens or hundreds of shorter exposures blended so trails connect while noise averages away. It's the approach the Twilightscapes camera is built around — start a stack, watch the trails accumulate live on screen, and stop when the circles satisfy you.

Face north to circle the trails around Polaris; face south and they arc like rainfall. And the Milky Way's bright core is a seasonal guest — roughly April through September in the northern hemisphere, hiding behind the sun the rest of the year. Plan accordingly.
