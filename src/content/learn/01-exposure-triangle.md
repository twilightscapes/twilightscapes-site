Every photograph is a negotiation with light, and at night there's very little of it to negotiate with. Three dials control the deal:

**Shutter speed** is how long the sensor drinks in light. In daylight you think in fractions of a second; at night you think in tens of seconds. Long shutters gather starlight — but they also record motion, which is either your enemy (a shaky tripod) or your entire art form (star trails, light painting).

**Aperture** is how wide the lens opens. A low ƒ-number like ƒ/2.8 is a wide-open eye — you want it open at night. Every stop you close costs you double the shutter time or double the ISO.

**ISO** is amplification. It makes a dim capture brighter, but it amplifies noise along with signal. Modern sensors handle ISO 3200 gracefully; older ones get gritty. Noise lives in the shadows — and at night, everything is shadows.

The trade is always the same: more shutter means motion, more aperture means shallower focus and softer corners, more ISO means noise. Night photography is choosing which compromise serves the picture.

Start with the recipe below on a clear night, far from city glow. Take the shot, then change exactly one dial and shoot again. Twenty minutes of that teaches more than any chart.
